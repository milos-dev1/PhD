Chapter 1

ConvLSTM-Based Iterative
Spectrogram Prediction for
Long-Horizon Vibration Forecasting

This chapter specifies a complete method for predicting future vibration spectrograms over long horizons (up to days) without attempting to jump days in a
single network call. A major window of vibration is converted to a spectrogram
by overlapping subwindow FFTs. A convolutional long short-term memory
network (ConvLSTM) consumes a short context of recent spectrograms and
predicts the next spectrogram only. That prediction is fed back as context,
and the same one-step model is applied again and again. Many such loops
compose a long-horizon forecast. The material is organized in two sections:
(1) spectrogram construction and the ConvLSTM one-step predictor; (2) iterative multi-step rollout for long-horizon forecasting. This document belongs
to the standalone spectrogram_prediction project and does not modify the
main doctoral thesis files.

5

1.1

Spectrogram Construction and ConvLSTM OneStep Prediction

Section 1 builds everything needed to predict

one next spectrogram: major windows,

STFT spectrograms, a short context buer, the ConvLSTM architecture, and one-step
training. Long-horizon iteration is deferred to Section 2.

1.1.1 Motivation and Scope
Industrial monitoring often needs awareness not only of the next few seconds but of
how spectral signatures may evolve over much longer periods. A single neural network
that directly outputs the spectrogram three days from now is poorly posed: operating
conditions change, and uncertainty explodes. A more controllable principle is:
1. learn a reliable one-step map from recent spectrograms to the next spectrogram;

2.

iterate that map: predict, append, predict again;

3. obtain a long horizon as the product of many short, physically meaningful steps.
This chapter adopts that principle with one architecture only: ConvLSTM. ConvLSTM extends LSTM by replacing gate matrix multiplications with convolutions, so the
hidden state remains a timefrequency feature mapexactly the geometry of a spectrogram.

1.1.8 Step 5  The ConvLSTM One-Step Predictor
Why ConvLSTM (Single Chosen Architecture)
This chapter does not oer a menu of networks.

ConvLSTM is the adopted model

because:

1. spectrograms are spatiotemporal maps;

2. long-horizon use needs memory of recent evolution;

11

3. iterative rollout needs a stable one-step sequence predictor;

4. convolutions preserve local harmonic structure inside the memory cell.

From Vanilla LSTM to ConvLSTM
A vanilla LSTM gate has the algebraic form

it = σ(Wxi xt + Whi ht−1 + bi ),

(1.10)

where xt and ht−1 are vectors and W(·) are dense matrices. If xt were a vectorized spectrogram, Wxi xt would mix every frequency bin with every other bin on every step, destroying
locality and exploding parameter count. ConvLSTM replaces those products by convolutions:

Wxi xt −→ Wxi ∗ Xt ,

(1.11)

so each output location depends only on a local neighborhood in timefrequency. That is
the decisive reason ConvLSTM is used on spectrograms.

Why two ConvLSTM layers?

One layer often underfits cross-window trends; deeper

than two rarely helps for k ≈ 3 and increases rollout drift.

Two layers are the default

compromise.

Why a convolutional stem before ConvLSTM?

Raw log-spectrograms mix sensor

noise with structure. A shallow stem computes local edge-like responses (harmonic lines,
bursts) so the recurrent memory stores features rather than raw pixels. This improves
one-step MSE in practice and is cheap.

Forward Pass in Words
1. Stack context Ut = (St−k+1 , . . . , St ).

2. Apply the shared conv stem to each context element.

3. Run ConvLSTM-1 then ConvLSTM-2 along the k steps; keep final states (H, C).

4. Decode H with conv layers to Ŝt+1 of shape (C, M, F ).

5. (Deploy) FIFO-insert Ŝt+1 into the buer for the next iteration.

Why teacher forcing first?

rollout

During training, feeding true past spectrograms teaches

the correct local dynamics before exposing the model to its own errors. For long rollout
deployment, scheduled sampling gradually replaces true context entries with predictions
so the network learns to correct small mistakes.

Scheduled Sampling Formula
At training step n, with probability pn feed the true spectrogram into the buer, and with
probability 1 − pn feed the network's own previous prediction:


pn = max pmin , p0 · η n ,

(1.22)

with defaults p0 = 1, η = 0.995, pmin = 0.7 for mild closing of the gap (or lower pmin when
aggressive rollout robustness is required).

16

Why schedule instead of pure teacher forcing forever?

Pure teacher forcing trains

a model that always sees perfect history; iterative day-scale deployment eventually sees
imperfect history. Scheduled sampling is the principled bridge.

1.1.10 Section 1 Summary
Section 1 defined the one-step problem end to end:

vibration windows become log-

magnitude spectrograms St via STFT; a context buer Ut feeds a two-layer ConvLSTM
with convolutional stem and decoder; training minimizes one-step MSE (optionally with
scheduled sampling). The deliverable of Section 1 is a trained map fθ : Ut 7→ Ŝt+1 with
identical input/output spectrogram shapes, ready for iterative reuse in Section 2.

Iterative Multi-Step Rollout for Long-Horizon Forecasting

Section 2 takes the trained one-step ConvLSTM and turns it into a long-horizon forecaster

1.2.1 Step 7  Iterative Feedback (The Long-Horizon Mechanism)

1. Predict Ŝt+1 = fθ (Ut ).

2. Append Ŝt+1 to the buer; drop the oldest entry (FIFO) so length stays k .

3. Set t ← t + 1.

4. Repeat until H future windows are produced.

1.2.8 Practical Deployment Modes
1.

Online hybrid: every real window refreshes the buer; ConvLSTM predicts only
the immediate next window for short lookahead.

2.

Gap filling: if acquisition pauses, iterate open-loop across the gap, then snap back
when data return.

3.

Scenario forecast: run H open-loop steps from the current buer to sketch possible spectral evolution (always labeled as forecast, not measurement).

21

Why prefer hybrid for days?

Because a day of continuous operation produces a

continuous stream of real windows. The iterative model is then repeatedly re-anchored
in truth, and long calendar time is covered by many accurate one-step predictions rather
than one fragile open-loop marathon.

Drift Control Strategies
Long open-loop chains drift. The chapter therefore standardizes three controls:

1.

Re-anchor period R: every R real windows, reset the buer to measured spectrograms (hybrid mode). Default R = 1 while streaming.

2.

Clamp / denoise predictions: optional mild Gaussian smoothing along frequency
before re-injection to suppress salt-and-pepper artifacts that explode under feedback.

3.

Horizon-wise validation: reject a configuration if validation MSE(h) exceeds a
threshold for h ≤ hok (e.g. hok = 30 for one minute).

Why not rely on a deeper network instead of re-anchoring?

Depth does not re-

move uncertainty about future loads and interventions. Re-anchoring uses new measurements
the only reliable information for day-scale coverage.
